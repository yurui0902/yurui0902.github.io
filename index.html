Research Project · NLP × History of Science

How the History of Science Is Narrated — A Comparative NLP Analysis

An NLP-driven historiographical comparison of how scholars writing on China and on the non-China world tell the story of modern science and technology.

Yu Rui May 2026 Project Proposal

The Question Behind the Project

The history of science and technology is now written by two scholarly communities whose intellectual centers of gravity look very different. One writes mostly about China; the other writes about everywhere else. They share a syllabus — Latour, Hughes, Bijker, Shapin, Pickering — but produce strikingly different stories about who makes knowledge, where it is made, and what counts as modern.

This project uses NLP at corpus scale to characterize that difference, and to do so in a way that is methodologically responsible — i.e., not "ask ChatGPT to summarize."

Two Synthesized Goals

Methodological. Demonstrate a verifiable path for AI/NLP in historical research, against the field's anxiety that "AI = ChatGPT-only."

Historiographical. Make a substantive contribution legible to the discursive style of contemporary China S&T history (Lei, Schmalzer, Mullaney, Seow, Bian, Bray).

The historiographical contribution is the proof of concept.

Two-Tier Research Design

The argument structure is progressive deepening: a broad descriptive landscape first, then a structural account of why the two sub-fields look that way. RQ1 surveys; RQ2 explains.

Research Question 1

Landscape Comparison — Descriptive

Four complementary lenses on what each sub-field actually talks about.

RQ1a · Topics

Topical Landscape

What topics each sub-field allocates attention to — shared, distinctive, and asymmetric.

MethodBERTopic on each sub-corpus, aligned via cross-corpus topic-centroid cosine similarity.
RQ1b · Theory

Cited Theoretical Anchors

Are both sub-fields taught with the same Western STS canon also writing with it identically? Or do their citation centers of gravity diverge?

MethodFuzzy name matching + NER over a curated ~50-author anchor list (STS canon, postcolonial theory, Asia-as-method).
RQ1c · Vocabulary

Distinctive Vocabulary

What words does each sub-corpus over-represent? The simplest, most robust signal.

MethodTF-IDF and log-likelihood keyness; top-N distinctive terms per side, embedding-clustered.
RQ1d · Extension

What They Call "Science"

What activity, object, or practice each sub-corpus actually names as science or technology — a preview of RQ2's deeper questions.

MethodDependency-parsed syntactic patterns: X is a science, scientific X, X technology.

Research Question 2

Narrative Habitus Comparison — Analytical

Three axes that, together, give a structural account of each sub-field's distinct way of telling history. The three map onto the canonical dimensions of historical narrative in social theory (Hayden White, Metahistory; William Sewell, Logics of History).

RQ2 axisSocial-theory dimensionWhat's at stake
Who is a knower? Agency Which actors enter history endowed with epistemic agency.
Where is knowledge made? Structure / institution The institutional matrix in which knowledge production is located.
What counts as modern? Temporality How history's direction, telos, and rupture get narrated.
Why three axes, not one

The axes mutually reinforce. The peasant knower of Maoist mass science is bound to the commune as its institution; "alternative modernity" (Schmalzer) and "co-produced modernity" (Lei) only make sense once you know who is co-producing it and where. Three independently estimated findings that converge constitute a robust claim, not three loose observations.

RQ2 · Who

Agency

Whose acts of knowing are syntactically foregrounded? Scientists, peasants, the state, women, translators, brokers, collectives?

MethodDependency-parsed subject — epistemic-verb pairs (know / discover / observe / experiment / invent / translate); NER + noun-phrase clustering on subjects.
RQ2 · Where

Institution

What institutional locations does the corpus identify as the venues of knowledge-making? Laboratory, university, commune, court, workshop, field station?

MethodSame pipeline as Who, swapping the target-term list to institutional NPs; location-pattern dependency extraction.
RQ2 · Modern

Temporality

Where does the word modern sit, semantically, in each sub-corpus? Is it nearer to "Western," "progressive," "rupture" — or to "co-produced," "translated," "alternative"?

MethodSub-corpus-specific word embeddings; Kozlowski projection of high-frequency nouns onto semantic axes (modern↔traditional, western↔eastern, progress↔backwardness).

How the Model Is Used

The pipeline is built so that every claim is traceable back to text. LLMs are kept in a verifiable, extractive role — they retrieve and quote, they do not summarize freely.

  • bge-large-en-v1.5Dense sentence/chunk embeddings — the shared semantic substrate for topic modeling, clustering, and retrieval.
  • BERTopicTopic discovery on each sub-corpus; topics are then aligned across sides by centroid similarity.
  • spaCyDependency parsing and NER — the engine for extracting subject–verb agency patterns and institutional noun phrases.
  • scipy · log-likelihoodKeyness analysis comparing the two sub-corpora; size-corrected distinctive-vocabulary lists.
  • gensim Word2VecPer-sub-corpus embedding spaces for Kozlowski-style semantic-axis projection of "modern."
  • RAG · structured extractFor each quantitative finding, retrieve exemplary chunks and prompt the LLM to return quoted sentences with source IDs — never paraphrase.
Validation Loop
1
Quantify. Statistical analysis produces a finding (e.g., "China sub-corpus over-represents peasant-actor terms by factor N").
2
Retrieve. RAG returns the top-k chunks that best exemplify the pattern.
3
Extract. A structured-output prompt forces the LLM to return verbatim quoted sentences and citations — not free-form summary.
4
Read closely. Manual verification and contextualization — the historian's interpretive labor where it belongs.

This satisfies the project's RAG-or-fine-tuning requirement while keeping the LLM in a role where its outputs are checkable against source text. Cf. Ayala & Bechard (NAACL 2024) on structured-output RAG; Düring et al. (Computational Humanities Research 2025) on Topic-RAG for historical corpora.

What a Finding Will Look Like

Illustrative Finding Shape

Both sub-fields draw heavily on Latour, Hughes, and Bijker as foundational STS theory, but the China sub-field cites postcolonial theorists — Said, Chakrabarty, Chen Kuan-Hsing — at significantly higher normalized rates. This indicates two different theoretical centers of gravity: the non-China sub-field anchors in internalist STS; the China sub-field anchors in STS plus postcolonial critique.

Each such finding is presented as: a quantitative claim, a figure or table, and a small set of verbatim quotations retrieved and verified through the RAG loop.

Why This Matters

The argument is not that NLP replaces close reading. It is that, at five-thousand-document scale, only a hybrid pipeline can do justice to the field's pluralism — surfacing patterns no single reader could see, while keeping every claim anchored in quotable, citable text. Done this way, AI in humanistic scholarship looks much less like the thing the field fears and much more like a long-overdue extension of the digital-humanities toolkit.
