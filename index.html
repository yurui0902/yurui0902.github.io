Same object. Different readings.

The core idea. The same concept, the same object, can be read in completely different ways depending on who's observing and from what context.

The Application

I'm applying this to the history of science and technology, comparing how China-based and non-China scholarship approach the same field — what they argue, what theories they use, what they call "science," and where they place emphasis.

Why This Matters

From "can AI?" to "how, responsibly?"

The history field is increasingly anxious about AI. The question isn't whether AI can serve as a research tool — it clearly can — but how to integrate it responsibly into humanities research.

My corpus is 5,000+ academic works, which no single scholar can read and synthesize by hand. So this project is a natural testbed for working out what AI-assisted humanities research could actually look like.

Research Question 1

How do China-based and non-China STS scholarship differ?

Three angles.

RQ1a · Topics

Topical Landscape

What do they write about? This surfaces shared concerns, China-distinctive topics, non-China-distinctive topics, and whether shared topics get equal weight on both sides.

MethodBERTopic on each sub-corpus with Qwen3-Embedding-8B as the underlying embedding; topics aligned across corpora by centroid cosine similarity.
RQ1b · Theory

Whose theory do they cite?

The Western STS canon is fairly well-defined — Hughes, Bijker, Pinch, Mumford, Edgerton, Marx, Latour — and the China sub-field is typically taught through this apparatus. The question is whether it's written through it the same way, or whether it leans on additional anchors like Asia as Method, Chen Kuan-Hsing, Lydia Liu, Prasenjit Duara, Partha Chatterjee.

MethodString match + NER, normalized by corpus size.
RQ1c · Extension

What counts as "science"?

What activity, object, or practice each sub-corpus actually names as science or technology.

MethodDependency-parsed syntactic patterns: X is a science, scientific X, X technology.
